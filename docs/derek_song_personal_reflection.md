# Personal reflection — Derek Song

My main responsibility was the exact branch-and-bound algorithm for minimum vertex cover. I also coordinated the comparison with my partner's approximation algorithm and ran the final experiment. I used ChatGPT and Codex extensively for explanations, implementation, testing, and writing support. My contribution included directing that work and interpreting and editing its outputs. I would distinguish this from independently writing all the code or personally discovering every issue.

The most important concept I learned was the difference between finding an optimal solution and proving that it is optimal. Initially, I focused mainly on whether the solver returned the smallest cover. Our results showed why its completion status matters. On the 24-vertex clique, the exact solver returned a cover of size 23 in every measured repetition, which is mathematically optimal, but still timed out. Its matching lower bound was only 12, so it had not eliminated every possibility of a smaller cover.

This also helped me understand the algorithm’s worst-case behaviour. Branching on an uncovered edge creates alternatives because every valid cover must include at least one endpoint. Although bounds can discard many branches, the search can still require exponential work. Increasing the number of vertices can therefore make proving optimality much more expensive. However, size alone was a poor predictor: the 40-vertex complete bipartite graph finished after only 21 visited states because its first complete cover matched its lower bound.

The design decision I found most challenging to understand was how to use bounds safely. A matching supplies a lower bound because its disjoint edges require distinct covering vertices. A feasible cover supplies an upper bound. Confusing these directions could incorrectly prune a branch containing the optimum. Explaining this distinction made branch and bound clearer to me than simply reading its pruning condition.

One development issue illustrated the limits of AI-generated code. The initial cover validator converted supplied identifiers into a set before validating them. Since Python treats `True` as equal to `1`, this could conceal an invalid Boolean identifier. Codex detected and corrected the issue and added regression tests. I did not discover this bug independently, but reviewing the explanation showed me why seemingly harmless input-processing choices can undermine validation.

If I repeated the project, I would spend more time manually tracing small examples before running the full experiment. I would also record my understanding and design decisions as the work progressed, rather than relying on a polished report to demonstrate them afterwards. For the solver itself, I would investigate stronger lower bounds as a separate experimental variant. Simply supplying an approximation-derived incumbent would not necessarily help: on even cliques, that approximation selects every vertex, just like our initial incumbent.

Overall, I developed a better understanding of what an exact algorithm guarantees and how to interpret its experimental results. A valid answer, an optimal answer, and a certified optimal answer are different claims. Keeping those distinctions clear was the most valuable lesson from the project.
